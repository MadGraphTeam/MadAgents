# 15 — Dispatch the what, not the how

> Give the consultant the goal and full context, but not the method. A presupposed diagnosis is the confident-recall trap, handed down.

Brief a delegate like a capable new hire: it knows the tools but not your goal, so give business
context, boundaries, and acceptance criteria — then leave the *method* to the specialist.
Over-detailed technical instructions cascade errors downstream (a planner should produce a
product spec, not an implementation plan). Hand over the symptom and let the source-grounded
agent locate the root cause; its read of the reliable source beats a presupposed answer.

**In a harness like this.** A dispatch carries the full sub-question, the verbatim ask, and the
paths, but leaves the technical method to the specialist. Crucially, the lead routes the
*question*, not a pre-decided answer — a presupposed diagnosis is exactly the confident-recall
trap (lesson 01) passed down the chain. The line to hold: naming *what to verify* is good
coverage (lesson 16); pre-supplying *the answer* is the trap to avoid.

## Where the books say it

- **Harness Engineering** — §08/§17 (dispatch the WHAT, not the HOW; over-detailed technical instructions cascade errors downstream; the Planner produces a product spec), §09 (consultant as a capable new hire — give business context, boundaries, acceptance criteria).
- **Claude Code: The Complete Guide** — §06. Describe symptoms rather than diagnosing causes; point to the reference or existing pattern rather than narrating it.
- **Codex Complete Guide** — §09. Name the traps the brief asks the consultant to *check* (bad input, empty content, timeout) — coverage, held in tension with not pre-supplying the answer.
